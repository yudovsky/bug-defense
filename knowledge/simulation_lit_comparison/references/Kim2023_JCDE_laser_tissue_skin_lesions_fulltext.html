Abstract

This study aimed to develop a simulation model that accounts for skin-specific properties in order to predict photothermal damage during skin laser treatment. To construct a computational model, surface geometry information was obtained from an optical coherence tomography image, and the absorption coefficient of the skin was determined through spectrophotometry. The distribution of the internal light dose inside the skin medium was calculated using the light propagation model based on the Monte Carlo method. The photothermal response due to the absorption of laser light was modeled by a finite difference time domain model to solve the bio-heat transfer equation. The predicted depth and area of the damaged lesions from the simulation model were compared to those measured in ex vivo porcine skin. The present simulation model gave acceptable predictions with differences of approximately ∼10% in both depth and area.

1. Introduction

Thermal treatment via laser irradiation has been widely used in various dermatological applications during recent decades (Anderson, 2000; Niemz, 2007), including skin rejuvenation and resurfacing (Bernstein et al., 1997; Heidari Beigvand, 2020), hair removal (Gan & Graber, 2013), vascular lesions (Landthaler & Hohenleutner, 2006), and thermal ablations (Miller, 2013; Welch et al., 1991). Heat transfer due to the absorption of irradiant energy from the laser pulse leads to various thermal reactions on the targeted skin area. The degree of thermal damage resulting from laser skin treatment is determined by several factors, such as the type of laser light beam, pulse duration, wavelength, and so on. One of the biggest challenges in optimal treatment planning is that the treatment outcome exhibits a considerable dependence on patient-specific characteristics such as geometric and optical properties of the treated skin area, pigmentations, skin type, etc.

Although the treatment guidelines for patient selection and laser protocol have been provided for safety, unexpected adverse reactions still occur in some patients. In order to reduce the risk of side effects, an appropriate selection of laser parameters is crucial for each treatment. Without the careful choice of the parameters for the laser treatment, additional thermal injuries due to overtreatment exceeding exposure limits or less ideal results due to insufficient thermal dose could arise. In most skin laser treatment procedures, however, it is not obvious for physicians to accurately diagnose the patient’s skin condition and control the laser device parameters considering the diverse and inconsistent patient-specific conditions. Without an accurate method to provide a prior estimation of damage extents over the treatment area, there is a significant chance for potential adverse effects. For the safety and improved efficacy of the laser treatment, predictive analysis to quantify the treatment outcome is significantly beneficial.

Computational modeling has proven its feasibility as a powerful tool in a wide range of biomedical engineering applications (Jean & Schulmeister, 2021; McMillan et al., 2021; Park et al., 2022; Yoon et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). The simulation models for predicting the laser–tissue interaction effects during laser irradiations generally include two subsequent models: light-tissue interaction and bio-heat transfer models. Light‐tissue interaction models are mainly divided into two different approaches, which are probabilistic and deterministic methods. A Monte Carlo (MC) method is a stochastic modeling technique based on the probability that each photon interacts with each other in the medium and is extensively used as a gold standard for light propagation simulations (Jacques & Pogue, 2008; Periyasamy & Pramanik, 2017). Deterministic models based on numerical methods, such as finite element analysis, are broadly used for fast evaluation and optimization as they generally incur less computational cost compared to the MC technique (Wagner et al., 2010). For analyzing heat transfer in biological tissues, various bio-heat transfer models have been developed (Andreozzi et al., 2019; Bhowmik et al., 2013; Jaunich et al., 2008; Pennes, 1948). Among these models, the Fourier-conduction-based Pennes’ bio-heat transfer equation (Pennes, 1948) is one of the most widely used models owing to its simplicity and feasibility.

Despite a number of studies focusing on light-tissue interactions and bio-heat transfer during laser treatment as individual phenomena, only a limited number of researches on the laser–tissue interaction simulations comprising both phenomena have been concurrently investigated (Crochet et al., 2006; Fadhali, 2015; Jiao & Guo, 2009; McMillan et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2019). To date, previous models have been focused on validations relying on idealized assumptions, such as flat skin surface domain with known optical properties. For the laser–tissue interaction simulation to be used in the actual medical practice, however, it is essential to take into account the patient’s individual data. There still remain computational challenges (i) to implement the simulation models that consider patient-specific characteristics such as skin conditions, skin geometry, optical properties, etc., and (ii) to utilize it in the treatment planning procedures.

In this paper, we propose a laser–tissue interaction simulation model that can consider skin-specific properties and aim to explore the potential of a predictive method for assisting skin laser treatment. The present model consists of three main steps: (i) skin surface modeling based on optical coherence tomography (OCT) image and spectrophotometry, (ii) light propagation simulation using the MC method, and (iii) bio-heat transfer simulation based on the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) algorithm. The model takes into account geometric, optical, and thermal parameters of the skin, pulse durations, wavelength, and profile of the laser beam. The accuracy of the proposed model is validated by comparing it to experimental results obtained using ex vivo porcine skin. 2. Computational Model

To construct a patient-specific computational model, the surface geometry information and the optical properties of the target tissue were obtained from the actual porcine skin specimen using OCT imaging technique and spectrophotometry, respectively. The distribution of the internal light dose inside the skin was calculated using the light propagation model based on MC techniques. After that, the thermal response due to the absorption of laser light was modeled by the FDTD method to solve Pennes’ bio-heat transfer equation. All the simulations were implemented in a graphic processing unit (GPU) device for expedited parallel processing using a personal computer (AMD Ryzen 9 3900 × 12-Core Processor @ 3.80 GHz, 64 GB memory and GeForce RTX 3090, 10 496 cores, 24 GB GDDR6X memory @ 936.2 GB/s bandwidth).

2.1. OCT-based skin geometry modeling

Four ex vivo skin specimens (named ‘PS 1’, ‘PS 2’, ‘PS 3’, and ‘PS 4’ herein) having dimensions of approximately 30 mm × 30 mm area with 5 mm thickness, were freshly excised from a mini pig (APURES, Pyeongtaek, Korea). Porcine skin is widely used as a substitute for human skin because of its similar anatomy and thermal response (Lavker et al., 1991; Sullivan et al., 2001). The prepared porcine skins were kept immersed in a physiological saline solution until being used (Fig. 2a). The skin surface was mechanically shaved to avoid any possible thermal/chemical damage. The bottom side of the porcine skin samples was fixed by adhesive to the 60 mm petri dish to maintain the exact location of the sample during both the imaging and laser irradiation (Fig. 2b).

Preparation of the porcine skin samples. (a) A sealed skin sample immersed in physiological saline solution and (b) the samples fixed on the petri dish.

The geometrical information of the porcine skin specimen was obtained using OCT imaging. OCT is one of the most widely used non-invasive optical imaging methods for both diagnosis and post-analysis (Fercher et al., 2003; Huang et al., 1991). The used OCT device in this study is the microscope-based custom-built spectral domain OCT with a central wavelength of 850 nm (T-850-HP-I, Superlum, USA) and a spectrometer with 2048 pixels of line scan camera (Cobra-S, Wasatch, USA). The objective lens positioners (MIPOS500, Piezojena, German) and linear actuators (LTA-HS, LTA-HL, ESP301, Newport, USA) were used for the exact positioning of each sample. For more detailed descriptions of an OCT system, see Kang et al. (2020).

Based on the high-resolution (0.0025 mm isotropic voxel resolution) cross-sectional image data collected from the OCT system, the three-dimensional (3D) surface geometry of each skin specimen was reconstructed. The 3D range of 2.5 mm × 2.5 mm × 0.75 mm of the porcine skin area was scanned by consecutive images (1000 × 300 size) of 1000 sampled cross-sections. For each specimen, two repetitive images were acquired regarding the same regions, and phase correlation-based image registration and averaging were performed to produce the noise-reduced image. The chromatic and scanning curvature distortion was corrected using numerical dispersion compensation and plane fitting. The spatial sampling period was adjusted isotropically before being used for the skin modeling procedure. Figure 3a and b, respectively, show an example of a cross-sectional image in the XZ-plane and a reconstructed 3D image.

Figure 3:
Open in new tabDownload slide

Exemplar OCT image of porcine skin (PS1). (a) Cross-sectional image and (b) 3D reconstruction of the skin surface.

The geometric information of the porcine skin surface was represented by the depth and surface normal vector at every voxel in the XY plane. The acquired OCT images were resampled to isotropic voxels of 0.005 mm using the Lanczos filter algorithm (Burger & Burge, 2009). The depth was measured as the distance from the top of the image to the voxel starting to have OCT intensity over 20. Then, Delaunay triangulation was performed using discrete points set representing the skin surface obtained from the depth information. The surface normal vector was obtained from the unweighted average of the normal vectors on adjacent triangular surfaces.

2.2. Optical properties of skin

For the light propagation simulation of skin tissues, we used the following optical parameters: absorption coefficient |${\mu }_\mathrm{a}$|⁠, scattering coefficient |${\mu }_\mathrm{s}$|⁠, refractive index |$n$|⁠, and anisotropy factor g. The skin absorption coefficient spectra were mainly dominated by water content, while scattering coefficient spectra showed a decreasing tendency with increasing wavelength (DeLisi et al., 2021; Zamora-Rojas et al., 2013). As the porcine skin specimens used in the experiment were submerged in saline water, we determine the absorption coefficient through direct measurement. On the other hand, the Nd: YAG laser we used has a single wavelength (1064 nm); we assumed that the scattering parameters would not be significantly affected by the types of porcine skin specimens. Therefore, the scattering coefficient with the corresponding refractive index and anisotropy factor was determined based on the literature values (Ding et al., 2005; Du et al., 2001; Jacques, 2013; Ma et al., 2005). To determine the absorption coefficient of the skin layer, the specimen was analyzed using a spectrophotometer (Cary 5000 UV-Vis-NIR system, Agilent Technologies-US). The reflectance rate for the skin was acquired for an optical range of 900–1300 nm as shown in Fig. 4. The physical quantity for the skin layer was evaluated based on Lambert’s law (Oshina, 2021):
$$\begin{eqnarray} I\left( x \right) = {I}_0{e}^{ - {\mu }_\mathrm{a}x} \end{eqnarray}$$
(1)
where |$I( x )$| is the light intensity at distance |$x$|⁠, |${I}_0$| is the light intensity at |$x\ = {\rm{\ }}0$|⁠, and |${\mu }_\mathrm{a}$| is the absorption coefficient. Given the average penetration depth of the 1064 nm laser beam (⁠|$d \approx 6\ {\rm{mm}}$|⁠) (Li et al., 2020; Shafirstein et al., 2007), the absorption coefficient was calculated using Eq. (1).

2.3. MC light propagation simulation

To simulate the photon propagation within the skin, a standard voxel-based multilayer MC simulation method was implemented. The size of the simulation domain was set to a cuboid of 2.0 mm × 2.0 mm × 1.5 mm, which sufficiently covers laser affected tissue region. The inside of the domain was discretized by 0.005 mm iso-voxels resulting grid size of 400 × 400 × 300. The air and skin regions were segmented using depth information from the processed OCT image as well as the normal vectors of the skin layer were assigned to the corresponding surface. Figure 5a shows an example of a segmented simulation domain. Considering the wavelength of 1060 nm used in this study, the optical parameters used for the skin tissue model were: |${\mu }_\mathrm{a} = 0.7512{\rm{\ c}}{{\rm{m}}}^{ - 1}$|⁠, |${\mu }_\mathrm{s} = 70{\rm{\ c}}{{\rm{m}}}^{ - 1}$|⁠, |$n\ = \ 1.4$|⁠, and g|$\ = \ 0.9$|⁠. For the air region, |${\mu }_\mathrm{a} = 0.0001{\rm{\ c}}{{\rm{m}}}^{ - 1}$|⁠, |${\mu }_\mathrm{s} = 0.0001{\rm{\ c}}{{\rm{m}}}^{ - 1}$|⁠, |$n\ = \ 1.0$|⁠, and g|$\ = \ 0.0$| were used to represent free photon movement without absorption and scattering.

Figure 5:
Open in new tabDownload slide

Light propagation simulation based on MC method. (a) Segmented simulation domain from the OCT image and (b) tracking process of the photon path in the tissue medium.

The laser source was modeled by referring to the actual laser device that we used in the experimental validation, an Nd: YAG laser (Q-switched Nd: YAG, New Wave Research). All the voxels inside of the disk with a diameter of 1.2 mm, placed on the center of the top surface of the simulation domain, were set to photon launch points. To represent the Gaussian beam profile, a different photon weight (W) to each source point was assigned using a distribution of |${e}^{ - 2r}$| in which r represents the normalized distance from the center of the source disk. 10 000 photons were launched for each of 45 213 source points, so 452 130 000 photons in total were demonstrated. The irradiation direction of photons was set to be normal to the XY plane of the simulation domain.

Each photon was subjected to a random walk model that iteratively simulates the procedure of (i) photon movement, (ii) absorption, and (iii) scattering based on the random selection of photon step size, scattering angle, and reflection or transmission at the boundary. In the solution procedure, a uniformly distributed random number (⁠|$\xi $|⁠) over the interval (0, 1) was repeatedly invoked by a computer.

In the calculation procedure of the photon movement, the position of the photon for the next iteration is determined by the current photon direction and step size (s):
$$\begin{eqnarray} s = \frac{{ - {\rm{ln}}\left( \xi \right)}}{{{\mu }_t}}\ {\rm{with\ }}{\mu }_t = {\mu }_\mathrm{a} + {\mu }_\mathrm{s}. \end{eqnarray}$$
(2)
When the photon passes through a boundary of the skin, the step size is divided into two parts, which are the step moving to the skin boundary (⁠|${s}_1$|⁠) and the remaining step size (⁠|${s}_2$|⁠). From the geometric skin information, |${s}_1$| was obtained, and the boundary interaction (reflection or transmission) was determined using the incident angle, the surface normal vector, and the generated random number, and then the photon was moved to the remaining step (Tran and Jacques, 2020; Wang et al., 1995). The remaining step size was calculated by
$$\begin{eqnarray} - \ln \left( \xi \right) = {\mu }_{t1}{s}_1 + {\mu }_{t2}{s}_2 \end{eqnarray}$$
(3)
where |${t}_1$| and |${t}_2$| are times corresponding to the step |${s}_1$| and |${s}_2$|⁠, respectively.
After arriving at the position of the next iteration, the photon undergoes absorption and scattering due to the interaction with the current media. The absorbed weight of the photon (⁠|$\Delta W$|⁠) was obtained by
$$\begin{eqnarray} \Delta W = \frac{{{\mu }_\mathrm{a}}}{{{\mu }_t}}W \end{eqnarray}$$
(4)
which was deposited in the local grid element as well as the photon weight was updated by
$$\begin{eqnarray} A\left( {ix,iy,iz} \right) \leftarrow A\left( {ix,iy,iz} \right) + \Delta W \end{eqnarray}$$
(5)
$$\begin{eqnarray} W \leftarrow W - \Delta W. \end{eqnarray}$$
(6)
Photon scattering was represented by statistically sampled two angles of a deflection angle (⁠|$\theta $|⁠) and an azimuthal angle (⁠|$\psi $|⁠) in which |$\theta $| was obtained from the Henyey–Greenstein function (Greenstein and Henyey, 1941; Wang et al., 1995) and |$\psi \ = \ 2\pi \xi $|⁠. Then, the new direction of the photon after scattering (⁠|$\mu {^{\prime}}_x$|⁠, |$\mu {^{\prime}}_y$|⁠, and |$\mu {^{\prime}}_z$|⁠) was calculated:
$$\begin{eqnarray} \mu {^{\prime}}_x = \frac{{\left( {{\mu }_x{\mu }_zcos\psi - {\mu }_ysin\psi } \right)}}{{\sqrt {1 - \mu _z^2} }}sin\theta + {\mu }_xcos\theta \end{eqnarray}$$
(7)
$$\begin{eqnarray} \mu {^{\prime}}_y = \frac{{\left( {{\mu }_y{\mu }_zcos\psi - {\mu }_xsin\psi } \right)}}{{\sqrt {1 - \mu _z^2} }}sin\theta + {\mu }_ycos\theta \end{eqnarray}$$
(8)
$$\begin{eqnarray} \mu {^{\prime}}_z = - \sqrt {1 - \mu _z^2} cos\psi sin\theta + {\mu }_zcos\theta \end{eqnarray}$$
(9)
where |${\mu }_x$|⁠, |${\mu }_y$|⁠, and |${\mu }_z$| are the directions of the photon before scattering. In the case that the photon direction was sufficiently close to the z-axis (i.e., |$| {{\mu }_z} | > 0.999\,99$|⁠), the following formulas were used:
$$\begin{eqnarray} \mu {^{\prime}}_x = sin\theta cos\psi \end{eqnarray}$$
(10)
$$\begin{eqnarray} \mu {^{\prime}}_y = sin\theta sin\psi \end{eqnarray}$$
(11)
$$\begin{eqnarray} \mu {^{\prime}}_z = SIGN\left( {{\mu }_z} \right)cos\theta \end{eqnarray}$$
(12)
in which |$SIGN( {{\mu }_z} )$| indicates 1 when |${\mu }_z$| is positive and −1 when |${\mu }_z$| is negative.

Figure 5b illustrates the tracking process of the photon path in the tissue medium. In the procedure of photon movement, absorption, and scattering were repeated until the weight of the photon decreases below a threshold value of 0.0001 or the photon is out from the simulation domain. After simulating 452 130 000 photons, the photon density in the simulation domain (⁠|$A$|⁠) was finally obtained.

2.2. Bio-heat transfer simulation

For heat transfer simulation caused by the absorption of laser light, the following modified Pennes bio-heat equation based on the enthalpy method (Abraham & Sparrow, 2007) is solved using the FDTD method in Cartesian coordinates:
$$\begin{eqnarray} \frac{{\partial {h}_t}}{{\partial t}} = \nabla \cdot \left( {{\kappa }_t\nabla T} \right) + {\rho }_b{c}_b{\omega }_b\left( {{T}_a - T} \right) + {Q}_{\mathrm{met}} + {Q}_{\mathrm{laser}} \end{eqnarray}$$
(13a)
$$\begin{eqnarray} {h}_t = \left\{ {\begin{array}{@{}*{1}{c}@{}} {{\rho }_l{c}_l\left( {T - {T}_i} \right)}\\ {{h}_t\left( {99} \right) + {h}_{fg}{C}_{\mathrm{ liq}}\left( {T - 99} \right)}\\ {{h}_t\left( {100} \right) + {\rho }_g{c}_g\left( {T - 100} \right)} \end{array}} \right.\ \begin{array}{@{}*{1}{c}@{}} {25^\circ C \le T \le 99^\circ {\rm{C}}}\\ {99^\circ {\rm{C}} < T \le 100^\circ {\rm{C}}}\\ {T >100^\circ {\rm{C}}} \end{array} \end{eqnarray}$$
(13b)
$$\begin{eqnarray} {\kappa }_t = \left\{ {\begin{array}{@{}*{1}{c}@{}} {{\kappa }_0 + \Delta \kappa \left( {T - {T}_i} \right)}\\ {{\kappa }_0 + \Delta \kappa \left( {100 - {T}_i} \right)} \end{array}} \right.\ \begin{array}{@{}*{1}{c}@{}} {T \le 100^\circ {\rm{C}}}\\ {T > 100^\circ {\rm{C}}} \end{array} \end{eqnarray}$$
(13c)
where |${h}_t\ ( {{\rm{J}}/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$| is the enthalpy per unit volume, |$t\ ( s )$| is the time, |${\kappa }_t{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{W}}/{\rm{m}}/{\rm{K}}} )$| is the thermal conductivity, |$T{\rm{\ }}( {\rm{K}} )$| is the tissue temperature, |${T}_i{\rm{\ }}( {\rm{K}} )$| is the initial temperature, |${\rho }_b{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{kg}}/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$| is the density of blood, |${c}_b{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{J}}/{\rm{kg}}/{\rm{K}}} )$| is the specific heat of blood, |${\omega }_b{\rm{\ }}( {1/{\rm{s}}} )$| is the blood perfusion coefficient, |${T}_a{\rm{\ }}( {\rm{K}} )$| is the arterial temperature, |${Q}_{\mathrm{met}}{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{W}}/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$| is the metabolic heat generation, and |${Q}_{\mathrm{laser}}{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{W}}/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$| is the heat production induced by laser irradiation. In Eq. 13(b), the phase change effects caused by water vaporization are taken into account in enthalpy form where |${\rho }_l$| and |${c}_l$| are the density and specific heat in the liquid phase, |${h}_{fg}$| is the latent heat of water (⁠|$2.17\ \times {10}^9\ {\rm{J}}/{{\rm{m}}}^{3}$|⁠), |${C}_{\mathrm{liq}}$| is the water content in the tissue, and |${\rho }_g$| and |${c}_g$| are the density and specific heat in the gas phase. The thermal conductivity is modeled as a linearly increasing function of temperature, as presented in Eq. 13(c), where |${\kappa }_0$| is the initial thermal conductivity. Because we simulated the ex vivo skin tissue, the metabolic heat generation rate and the blood perfusion were not considered in the model. For the present simulation model, the initial density of the porcine specimen for the liquid phase was measured, and the heat capacity, the thermal conductivity, and the fraction of water in the tissue were determined based on literature data (Crochet et al., 2006; Fadhali, 2015; Xu et al., 2008). The used thermal properties are: |${\rho }_l = 1,109\ ( {{\rm{kg}}/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$|⁠, |${c}_l = 3,391\ ( {{\rm{J}}/{\rm{kg}}/{\rm{K}}} )$|⁠, |${\rho }_g = 4,400\ ( {{\rm{kg}}/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$|⁠, |${c}_g = 382\ ( {{\rm{J}}/{\rm{kg}}/{\rm{K}}} )$|⁠, |${C}_{\mathrm{liq}} = 0.75$|⁠, |${\kappa }_0 = 0.37{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{W}}/{\rm{m}}/{\rm{K}}} )$|⁠, |$\Delta \kappa \ = {\rm{\ }}0.0022{\rm{\ }}( {{\rm{W}}/{\rm{m}}/{\rm{K}}} )$|⁠, and the initial temperature of the tissue was set to be |$25^\circ {\rm{C}}.$| The heat source due to the laser energy is evaluated as
$$\begin{eqnarray} {Q}_{\mathrm{laser}} = PA \end{eqnarray}$$
(14)
where |$P\ ( {\rm{W}} )$| is the delivered laser power and |$A\ ( {1/{{\rm{m}}}^3} )$| is the density of the absorbed photon per each voxel (Welch and van Gemert, 2011).
In order to calculate temperature distribution, the bio-heat equation is numerically solved by employing the FDTD algorithm. Based on the FDTD algorithm, the temperature field is discretized as
$$\begin{eqnarray} &&{\frac{{{\rho }_{i,j,k}{c}_{i,j,k}}}{{\Delta t}}\left( {^{t + \Delta t}{T}_{i,j,k} - \,^t{T}_{i,j,k}} \right)}\nonumber\\&&\quad={ \frac{1}{{\Delta {s}^2}}\left[ {\kappa \left( {{T}_{i + 1,j,k} - {T}_{i,j,k}} \right) - \kappa \left( {{T}_{i,j,k} - {T}_{i - 1,j,k}} \right)} \right]}\nonumber\\&&\qquad+{ \frac{1}{{\Delta {s}^2}}\left[ {\kappa \left( {{T}_{i,j + 1,k} - {T}_{i,j,k}} \right) - \kappa \left( {{T}_{i,j,k} - {T}_{i,j - 1,k}} \right)} \right]}\nonumber\\&&\qquad+{ \frac{1}{{\Delta {s}^2}}\left[ {\kappa \left( {{T}_{i,j,k + 1} - {T}_{i,j,k}} \right) - \kappa \left( {{T}_{i,j,k} - {T}_{i,j,k - 1}} \right)} \right] + Q_{i,j,k}^{\mathrm{Laser}}} \end{eqnarray}$$
(15)

3. Experimental Validation

3.1. Laser irradiation

The experimental setup used for the laser irradiation procedure on ex vivo porcine skin is presented in Fig. 6. A nanosecond Nd: YAG pulsed fiber laser (Biolino MOPA, Laservall), which provides a peak power of 20 W, repetition rates of 1.6–1000 kHz, and spot size 1.2 mm, was used for the experiment. In all experiments, the laser was used after measuring the laser output using a thermal sensor power meter (Nova II, Ophir). To measure the power, the laser power meter was located directly on the specimen before the irradiation procedure. The skin specimens were divided into two groups and were irradiated with pulse duration of 120 ns (PS 1 and PS 2) and 480 ns (PS 3 and PS 4), respectively. For all the specimens, 15 W power and frequency at 1000 kHz were used. In order to minimize tissue shrinkage and deformation due to moisture drying, the damaged area was measured immediately after the laser was processed.

3.2. Post-processing for damage evaluation

After the laser irradiation process, the ex vivo porcine skin specimens were subjected to post-processing procedures to evaluate the damage extent caused by laser–tissue interaction. The specimens were first imaged with an optical microscope (OLS5000, Olympus) to obtain the surface image of the irradiated area. The imaging was conducted for each specimen by acquiring images parallel to the direction of the laser beam. Next, the specimens were sectioned using a surgical knife perpendicular to the upper surface, with respect to the center of the laser treatment area. Both the surface and side-view images were analyzed to determine the coagulated and ablated regions of the tissue. The post-processing procedure was conducted independently to ensure the accuracy of the analysis.

The coagulation and ablation regions were evaluated using an image processing program (ImageJ, IBM). The images were first converted to grayscale, where each pixel was assigned a gray intensity value ranging from 0 (black) to 255 (white). A threshold value was then determined using a graph of gray intensity values versus the number of pixels. In this graph, the gray intensity value of the non-laser treated normal tissue area occupied 90% of the total area, and the remaining 10% of the gray values were selected as the coagulated area affected by laser interaction. Figure 7 shows the detailed procedure to determine the coagulated areas. For the ablation regions, the same procedure was used, but the pixels with the gray intensity value of the lower 2% value were selected as the ablated area. Note that the hair parts in the images were excluded from the area selection. Figure 8 shows the detailed procedure to determine the ablated areas.

4. Results

4.1. Damage evaluation

Figure 9 illustrates photothermal reactions due to the laser energy absorbed in the biological tissue. The locally generated heat by the irradiant energy is transferred radially to the surrounding areas, resulting in different thermal effects depending on the irradiation time and peak temperature: hyperthermia (∼45°C), coagulation (∼|$60^\circ {\rm{C}}$|⁠), vaporization (⁠|$100^\circ {\rm{C}}$|⁠), and carbonization (>|$200^\circ {\rm{C}}$|⁠) (Niemz, 2007; Schneider & Feussner, 2017; Zhang et al., 2022). For the temperature range in hyperthermia, the photothermal effect is reversible. Once the tissue temperature exceeds |$60^\circ {\rm{C}}$|⁠, irreversible tissue damages, such as coagulation and thermal necrosis occur. Carbonization occurs when laser irradiation continues after the tissue moisture content has evaporated. For the purpose of comparing the simulation model and experimental results, we evaluated two different stages of thermal interactions, i.e., coagulation and ablation. In the simulation model, the coagulated and ablated regions were evaluated based on the temperature threshold 55°C and |$200^\circ {\rm{C}}$|⁠, respectively.

5. Discussion

In this paper, we propose a simulation model that accounts for skin-specific to predict photothermal damage during skin laser treatment. To model the surface of the porcine skin specimen, the geometric information of the skin was acquired from the OCT system. The absorption coefficient of skin layer for the simulation model was determined through spectrophotometry. The scattering coefficient with the corresponding refractive index and anisotropy factors were determined based on the literature values (Ding et al., 2005; Du et al., 2001; Jacques, 2013; Ma et al., 2005). The distribution of the internal light dose inside the skin was calculated using the light propagation model based on MC techniques. After then, the thermal response due to the absorption of laser light was modeled by the FDTD model to solve Pennes’ bio-heat transfer equation. The simulation model was compared with the experimental results using Nd: YAG pulsed laser and ex vivo porcine skin. The irradiated skin specimen was cut through the central section of the laser treatment area, and the damaged region was measured for comparison with the simulation model.

In skin laser irradiation, the degrees of thermal reactions depend not only on the type of laser protocols but also on the geometric property of the target skin area. The simulation results showed that the distribution of the photons and the resulting temperature distribution due to laser irradiation are considerably affected by the geometry of the skin surface. Therefore, to evaluate the accurate internal light dose of the irradiated skin area, it is important to consider the geometric information of the target region. Unlike previous studies that assumed the skin surface as a flat domain (Fadhali, 2015; Jiao & Guo, 2009; McMillan et al., 2021), the actual 3D geometry of the skin surface was obtained using the OCT system for more precise evaluations. The differences between the simulation and experiment results in average depth and estimated area for coagulation damage were |$7.2{\rm{\% }}$| and |$8.6{\rm{\% }}$|⁠, respectively, and for ablation lesions were |$1.9{\rm{\% }}$| and |$4.3{\rm{\% }}$|⁠, respectively, indicating that the predictive modeling capability of the present simulation model is acceptable.

Although the present model demonstrates its feasibility to predict photothermal damage during skin laser irradiation, there still remains further work needs to be done to improve the model. First, the absorption coefficient was measured through a spectrophotometer, whereas the scattering coefficient with the corresponding refractive index and anisotropy factor was determined based on the literature (Crochet et al., 2006; Fadhali, 2015; Xu et al., 2008). For the heat-transfer simulation model, the initial density of the specimen for the liquid phase was measured, but the heat capacity, the thermal conductivity, the density for the gas phase, and the fraction of water in the tissue were determined based on literature data (Crochet et al., 2006; Fadhali, 2015; Xu et al., 2008). Because direct measurements of all the skin-specific properties are challenging due to various limitations, including cost and equipment availability, we made a careful decision to measure the most crucial properties and supplement the rest with literature values in order to achieve reasonable accuracy. Future studies aiming to address these limitations would further enhance the skin-specificity of the model. Second, to model the thermal responses in the skin layers, the present model assumes that the stage of the thermal reaction proceeds continuously with increasing temperature. The current model could be further improved by including the advanced damage growth model for the thermal injury mechanisms in skin layers. Another limitation is that the current model did not concern laser scattering induced by local spots and hairs in the skin layers. Because the absorption of light is considerably affected by the optical properties, the inclusion of the varying optical properties of the skin layer into the model could decrease the difference between the simulations and experiments. At last, the laser–tissue interactions in a living skin tissue remain to be investigated as the effect of the metabolism and vessel system of in vivo tissues will obviously also affect the degree of thermal responses.

In the experimental procedure, the laser irradiation process of the porcine skin specimen was performed after OCT imaging to obtain the skin geometry information. For the exact positioning on the center of each skin specimen, the objective lens positioners and linear actuators were used during the OCT imaging process, and a 3D printed holder was used during the laser irradiation. However, in the manual maneuvering process of transferring the specimen from the OCT system to the laser device, an inadvertent discrepancy may have occurred between the central positions defined in the OCT imaging process and the positions where the actual laser beam was irradiated. If the experimental process is carried out in equipment integrating skin scanning and laser irradiation, it will be possible to reduce the error between simulation and experimental results.

In this study, we used a 1060 nm nanosecond Nd: YAG for the experimental comparisons, which indicates that the validations were limited to the specific laser irradiation protocol. Since the laser treatment outcomes are affected by several laser device factors, such as power, beam type, duration of irradiation, and wavelength, the proposed model needs to be verified with various laser treatment protocols. In addition, the current model could be further verified with more experimental comparisons with skin samples from multiple individuals. Finally, given that laser treatment has been largely used not only for cosmetic purposes but for various skin cancer treatments, it would be valuable to investigate the thermal reactions, including the malignant tissue model.

6. Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a laser–tissue interaction simulation model for predicting photothermal damage lesions during skin laser treatment that takes into account skin-specific properties. The simulation model utilized the OCT system to acquire the geometric information of the skin and spectrophotometry to determine the absorption coefficient of the skin layer. The distribution of the internal light dose inside the skin was calculated using the light propagation model based on MC techniques. The thermal response due to the absorption of laser light was modeled by the FDTD method to solve the modified Pennes’ bio-heat transfer equation. To validate the simulation model, experiments were conducted using ex vivo porcine skins and a 1060 nm Q-switched Nd: YAG laser. The results showed that the present simulation model exhibits acceptable predictive modeling capability in both the depth and area of the damaged regions compared to the experimental measurements. For more comprehensive skin-specific modeling, future studies could build upon our work to address complete skin properties. With an improved system combining the computational model with a non-invasive modality for obtaining skin characteristics, the model could offer prognostic insights during laser treatment.
